01 01 The premise

Every backtest is a liar

Hobbyist trading systems die the same death: a backtest — a replay of a strategy's rules over historical data — that flatters. Look-ahead bias (trading on information the strategy couldn't have had yet), costless, perfect fills (order executions at prices you'd never actually get), parameters tuned until the curve looks heroic — then real money meets a strategy that never existed. I didn't want to build another hunt for market-beating returns. I wanted to build the system that cannot lie to me, and treat that as the engineering problem.

So Trading Lab is paper-trading-first by construction. Every strategy climbs a promotion ladder — backtest to walk-forward (re-fit on a rolling window, judged only on the unseen data that follows) to paper trading (live market data, simulated money) to gated live — with demotion automatic at every rung; the figure below has the gates.

The scope is deliberately boring: end-of-day (EOD) daily bars, long-only, three liquid US ETFs (SPY, QQQ, IWM). The strategies are textbook on purpose — a 50/200 SMA cross (hold while the 50-day moving average sits above the 200-day) and an RSI-2 dip buyer (a two-day relative-strength index, buying short-term oversold dips) — because the system is the product, not the alpha. Most strategy ideas fail; that's the expected outcome. The durable asset is the infrastructure that makes the next idea cheap to test honestly: the engine, the cost model, the risk layer, the journal.

The repo's design doc states the operating principle outright, and it drove every decision below: a misleading number is a worse bug than a missing feature.

02 02 The engine

One simulation core, zero forks

I wrote a custom simulation engine — roughly 500 lines — instead of adopting backtrader or vectorbt. For an EOD problem this small, a framework buys convenience and costs explainability; I wanted every number in every report traceable to code I can read in one sitting.

The architectural bet that matters most: one SimulationCore runs both backtesting and paper trading. The daily step is identical — fill queued orders at the open, check stops, mark equity (value the account at that day's prices), evaluate the kill switch, queue new orders. Because the code path is shared, any divergence between a paper account and a backtest replay over the same days is a data problem, not a code fork — the same reproducible-from-the-log bar I hold the event-streamed systems in my day job to. The parity test — test_paper_incremental_equals_continuous_replay — is the most important test in the repo: it proves that daily incremental paper runs, including missed-day catch-up, produce exactly the state one continuous replay produces.

No-look-ahead is enforced by the engine, not by strategy discipline. A signal computed at bar t's close fills at bar t+1's open, pinned by test_no_lookahead_fill_at_next_open. Stops model a resting stop order — a standing instruction to sell once price falls to a set level: if the market opens already below the stop (a gap), the fill is at that worse open; if price merely touches the stop intraday, the fill is at the stop price.

Costs are pessimistic by default — max($1, $0.005/share) commission plus 5 bps of slippage per side (basis points, hundredths of a percent — the gap between the screen price and the fill you actually get). A zero-cost run requires deliberately editing config. Optimism should never be an accident.

03 03 The risk layer

Strategies are not allowed to touch money

A strategy in Trading Lab emits exactly one thing: desired exposure, 0 or 1, per bar at the close. It cannot size a position, place an order, or see the account. A separate risk layer converts intent into shares:

  • 0.5% of current equity risked per trade against an ATR stop (average true range — how far the ticker typically moves in a day; the stop distance sets the share count, so size is an output of risk, never an input).
  • A 25% position cap, so a tight stop can never justify a huge position.
  • Five concurrent positions max — correlations converge to one in selloffs.
  • No shorting or leverage anywhere in the interfaces.

Capability absence beats capability discipline.

Drawdown — the fall from peak account equity — gets a two-tier, software-enforced response. At −10% from peak, new entries pause while existing positions keep their stops and exits. At −15%, the kill switch liquidates everything and halts the account until a manual, deliberate reset — it fires precisely when my judgment would be worst, which is the point. Both tiers run identically in backtest, in the internal paper simulator, and against the adapter for Alpaca, a retail brokerage with a paper-trading API.

About that adapter: it is paper-only by construction. There is no live-trading switch in the codebase to be careful with. The broker executes and reports; my code decides. And the permanent internal simulator keeps running in parallel as a shadow reference, so fill quality against a real broker's paper engine is itself measured data.

04 04 The adversarial review

Five critics, one verdict

After v1 shipped, I ran a structured adversarial review: five simulated trader personas — a full-time day trader, an intermediate swing trader, a long-term investor, a beginner, and a professional systematic PM — each driven through the real tool, pushed past its limits, with a critique filed per persona. The critiques and their synthesis live in the repo as research docs.

All five, including the two whose trading styles the tool can't serve at all, independently reached the same verdict: the engine is honest and the reports are not. The plumbing earned trust everywhere it was probed — next-open fills, resting-stop semantics, pessimistic costs, the holdout guard (a final slice of history you may test against exactly once), deterministic reruns, full run provenance. But every persona's worst finding was output that misleads rather than a feature that's missing. The headline: no report anywhere compared a strategy to buy-and-hold — just buying the index and doing nothing — while both shipped strategies lag SPY by double-digit CAGR points (compound annual growth rate) on the tool's own cached data. The reports framed them in flattering absolute terms instead. The beginner persona put it brutally: they would have paper-traded for six months believing it worked.

That verdict inverted the roadmap. For a project whose entire value is trustworthy evidence, the highest-impact work was not adding capability — it was making existing capability tell the truth. I rebuilt the roadmap into honesty-first waves, and the benchmark layer shipped before any new feature did. Getting told your reports flatter you, by your own review process, and reordering everything in response: that's the system working on its own author. It is also how everything I build gets reviewed: independent adversarial lenses pushed through the real artifact, every finding triaged — fixed or rejected with a written reason — before anything ships.

05 05 The honesty layer

Reports that argue against themselves

The honesty wave turned the critique into machinery.

No number stands alone. Every backtest and walk-forward report now carries a benchmark block: buy-and-hold SPY and an equal-weight universe portfolio over the identical window, same adjusted data, same cost model — with alpha and beta (return in excess of the benchmark, and how hard the strategy moves when the benchmark moves), capture ratios (the share of the benchmark's up and down moves you participate in), and a plain-English verdict line. A strategy that lags the index says so in its own report. Both shipped strategies currently do, openly. That sentence printing is the thesis of the whole project.

The ladder became executable. tradinglab gate <run-dir> evaluates the promotion criteria as PASS/FAIL from run artifacts — trade count, expectancy after costs (average profit per trade), margin over the modeled round-trip cost, robustness with the best trades removed, the benchmark verdict. Promotion is no longer prose; it's an exit code.

Expectation bands with path-based rules. tradinglab envelope builds block-bootstrap expectation bands (resampling contiguous chunks of the trade record, so streaks survive the shuffle) from a validated record and judges the live paper account against them — path-based, so a crash below the kill percentile (the pre-registered worst-case path) that later bounces still kills. No getting lucky retroactively.

Regime splits. Every backtest reports performance across benchmark trend × volatility terciles (thirds), so an edge concentrated in one friendly regime can't hide in the average.

Loud rejection of bad inputs. Intraday or NAV-only data (net asset value), negative costs, and off-contract signals all raise clear errors instead of producing confident wrong answers.

06 06 Status

What the ladder has promoted so far: nothing

Where it stands, stated plainly. The suite is 108 tests, with the backtest/paper parity test at its center. Two textbook strategies have full backtest and walk-forward records with parameter surfaces (performance mapped across the whole grid of parameter choices) and out-of-sample expectancy persisted per walk-forward window. The internal simulator runs the daily paper cycle; the Alpaca paper adapter runs the same signals against a real broker's paper engine in parallel, with the simulator as shadow reference — the divergence between them is exactly the fill-quality data the paper rung exists to measure. A view-only web dashboard built over the same run artifacts and journal puts the benchmark blocks, expectation envelopes, and gate verdicts on one screen, so the paper account is watched visually, not just through the CLI.

And no strategy has been promoted to real money. Not because the ladder is unfinished — because the gates work. The benchmark verdict says both strategies lag buy-and-hold, so they sit at the paper rung generating fidelity data, and gate will keep saying FAIL until something earns PASS. A system that promoted them anyway would be a more impressive demo and a worse piece of engineering.

Ahead, in order: first-class user strategies and a universe scan command, then an event-style signal contract. Today a strategy can only say “be in” or “be out” — so after a stop ejects a position, the strategy still says “be in” and re-buys the next day, paying the round-trip cost again. That churn is the one structural cost I've documented rather than hidden; entering only on a fresh cross (plus trailing stops and shorting) removes it. The ladder is also ML-ready by construction: a learned model is just another strategy emitting 0-or-1 exposure, and it gets no shortcuts — the same walk-forward windows, the same one-shot holdout, the same regime splits, the same gate. And the lab itself is built the way everything I ship is built: directed agent pipelines under an agent contract file in the repo, with the test suite deciding what lands. The repo is private; this page is the tour.

The summary: I set out to build a system that couldn't lie to me, and the proof it works is that it keeps delivering news I don't want. Every claim on this page traces to a test, a report, or a critique in the repo. That's the job.

07 07 What this demonstrates

Three pillars, receipts attached

Distributed systems

Replay determinism, proven — incremental paper runs produce exactly the state of one continuous replay, pinned by the parity test. It's the same event-sourcing property that makes event-streamed systems debuggable — the bar I hold the ones in my day job to.

AI engineering

Five LLM-simulated trader personas, driven through the real tool, filed the critique that inverted the roadmap. The ML-ready harness — walk-forward windows, a one-shot holdout, regime splits — is what any learned strategy must climb, in a lab developed by directed agent pipelines.

Full-stack

A view-only web dashboard over the run artifacts — benchmark blocks, expectation envelopes, and gate verdicts on one screen — beside the CLI reporting surface.
